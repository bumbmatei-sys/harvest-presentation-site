There is a clipboard by the door of a lot of small churches. It has a sheet on it with four columns — name, phone, email, first time? — and by the end of the service it has eleven rows, three of them illegible, one of them a child's name written by a child. Somebody carries it to the office. It goes in a drawer with the other ones.

Nothing about that is anyone's fault. The welcome desk is staffed by a volunteer who also has to find the nursery, and the clipboard is genuinely the cheapest thing that works on a Sunday morning. The problem is what happens on Monday, which is nothing. The sheet is not data. It is a photograph of a room, and a blurry one.

So the useful question is not "how do we get people to sign in?" — they already do. It is: what has to happen for the eleventh row on that sheet to still exist in March, attached to a person, next to everything else you know about them?

## What a check-in system has to do

Before you evaluate any tool, it helps to know what the job actually is. Four things, in order of how often they get skipped:

**It has to be faster than the clipboard.** If a first-time visitor has to download an app, create an account, verify an email and set a password, they will not do it. They will walk past the desk. A check-in flow that asks more of a stranger than paper does is worse than paper, because paper at least gets filled in.

**It has to work for somebody who has never been there before.** A flow built around looking up an existing record has nothing to offer the people you most want to capture. On a Sunday with visitors, half the point is the people who are not in the system yet.

**The list has to leave the room.** A live count on a screen is nice on the day and useless by Tuesday. Whatever captures the names has to hand them to whatever holds your people long-term, without a human retyping anything.
