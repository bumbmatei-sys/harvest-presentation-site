Most ministries meet the same question eventually, and it almost never arrives as a question. It arrives as a Sunday when the one person who knows where the key is kept is ill. It arrives as a family in crisis who will only speak to the leader who happens to be away that week. Sometimes it arrives as a resignation, and a room of good people discovering they have no real idea how anything worked.

None of that is a failure of effort. It is usually the opposite. It is what happens when a faithful person carries more than one person can carry, for long enough that the carrying becomes the structure.

Scripture treats that pattern as a problem to be solved rather than a virtue to be admired. When Moses' father-in-law watched him sitting as judge over Israel from morning until evening, his response was blunt: "What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to do it alone" (Exodus 18:17–18, ESV). The remedy was not for Moses to work harder or to care less. It was to appoint able men over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens, so that the ordinary matters were handled by many and only the hardest cases came to him.

Two things in that account are easy to skim past. The first is that Jethro's concern ran in both directions: *you and the people with you.* The people waiting in line from morning to evening were wearing out too. A bottleneck is not only exhausting for the person who is the bottleneck; it is quietly costly for everyone standing on the other side of it. The second is that nothing in the arrangement suggested Moses had been doing the work badly. He was doing it well. That was precisely the problem — he was doing it so well that no one else had learned to.

The same logic shows up in the early church at the moment it faced its first internal conflict. Some widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution of food, and the twelve did not respond by adding hours to their week. "It is not right that we should give up preaching the word of God to serve tables," they said, and the gathering chose seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom, whom the apostles then appointed to the task. The apostles' own summary of what they were protecting is one line: "But we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word" (Acts 6:2, 4, ESV).

Notice what was handed over. Not the trivial work — the daily distribution was the very thing the complaint was about, urgent enough to threaten the unity of the church. It was serious, visible, and it mattered to real people who were going hungry. They gave away the pressing thing and kept the thing that could not be delegated without the church losing something essential. That is a harder discipline than giving away the jobs nobody wants.

Underneath both accounts is a settled conviction about what leaders are for. Christ gave the church its leaders — "the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ" (Ephesians 4:11–12, ESV). *Equip* is doing the work in that sentence. The measure of the office is not how much ministry passes through the leader's hands, but how much ministry the congregation is now able to do. By that measure, a leader who does everything is not succeeding twice over; they are succeeding at one thing and failing at the other.

Paul put the same idea in terms of time rather than structure when he told Timothy to take what he had heard "in the presence of many witnesses" and "entrust to faithful men, who will be able to teach others also" (2 Timothy 2:2, ESV). Count the generations in that sentence: Paul, Timothy, the faithful, and others after them. Four, in a single clause, and most of them Paul would never meet. He was writing near the end of his life about people whose names he did not know.

That is the shape of it, and it is worth saying plainly that the shape is expensive up front. Handing work to someone else costs more before it costs less. The first several times another person leads the meeting or handles the visit or reconciles the accounts, it takes longer, and the result is usually worse than it would have been. Anyone who has tried it knows the temptation to take it back, and the reasoning always sounds responsible: it is faster this way, and the people deserve the better version. The reasoning is also true, in the short run. It just quietly guarantees that in five years the work will still depend entirely on one person, who by then will be tired.

The obstacle, more often than not, is not doubt about other people's competence. It is what being needed provides. Being the one who is called, the one who knows, the one without whom the thing does not happen — that meets something real in a person, and it is not always easy to distinguish from faithfulness. It is worth examining honestly, because a leader who needs to be needed will keep finding sound reasons not to share the load.

Two practical consequences follow. Competence that lives only in one person's head leaves when that person does, so anything that would be lost in a sudden absence is worth writing down, however unspiritual that feels. And succession is not an event at the end of a tenure but a habit maintained throughout it, because the alternative is starting the conversation at the worst possible moment.

The church has outlasted every leader it has ever had. That is not a comment on any of them; it is the nature of the thing. Whatever is built to depend on one particular person is, whatever else is also true of it, temporary. Building as though you will be replaced is not pessimism about your ministry. It is the assumption the New Testament makes about all of them.
