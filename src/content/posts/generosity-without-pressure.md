Every church has to talk about money, and almost nobody enjoys it. The subject arrives with its own weather — a small change in the room, a shuffle, the sense that something is about to be asked. Leaders feel it before the congregation does, which is probably why it so often ends up in one of two ditches. Either it barely happens at all, or it happens with the volume turned up.

Silence looks like the safer ditch. It is not. A church that never speaks about money has not avoided teaching about money; it has taught that the subject is faintly embarrassing and that faith and finance belong in separate rooms. Meanwhile the congregation is being instructed about money constantly by everything else in their week, and none of that instruction is neutral. Refusing to speak does not create a vacuum. It concedes the ground.

The other ditch is more tempting, because it works. Urgency, a deadline, a running total on a screen, an appeal timed for the moment when feeling is highest — these are effective. They raise money. They also do something else, and the something else is the reason to be careful.

Paul puts the decision somewhere very specific: "Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7, ESV). *Has decided.* The verb has already happened by the time the offering comes round. The decision belongs to the giver, formed in private, before the room and its atmosphere ever got involved. Any technique whose purpose is to produce a decision in the moment is, whatever its intentions, working against that sentence rather than with it.

Compulsion is the obvious violation, and few churches would defend it outright. Reluctance is subtler and easier to cause by accident. A person can give without being coerced and still walk out flat — because they gave to stop feeling watched, or because a number was named that they could not manage and no one said the smaller amount was welcome. Comparison is the quiet engine of most manipulation, and Paul disarms it directly: "For if the readiness is there, it is acceptable according to what a person has, not according to what he does not have" (2 Corinthians 8:12, ESV). That is a sentence for the person in the pew who has almost nothing to give and has been made to feel it. It is also a boundary on what a leader may reasonably imply.

He is careful about his own posture too. Of the whole collection he says, "I say this not as a command, but to prove by the earnestness of others that your love also is genuine" (2 Corinthians 8:8, ESV). He wants the gift. He tells them so. He declines to convert wanting it into requiring it.

Jesus watched an offering once. Rich people were putting in large sums; a poor widow put in two small copper coins. "Truly, I say to you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the offering box. For they all contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on" (Mark 12:43–44, ESV). The arithmetic runs on proportion, not on totals — worth remembering where a handful of gifts fund most of the budget and everyone else can feel like a rounding error.

Notice also who heard it. He called his disciples over and said it to them. She was not summoned to the front, not named, not held up as a rebuke to the people with money. The commendation was real and she never had to carry it in public.

Then there is the plumbing, which is less inspiring and more important. Paul travelled with a delegation the churches themselves had appointed, and said why: "We take this course so that no one should blame us about this generous gift that is being administered by us, for we aim at what is honorable not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of man" (2 Corinthians 8:20–21, ESV). Not only in the Lord's sight. He is not content with a clean conscience; he wants an arrangement that looks clean to sceptical observers, because a gift handled without visible accountability damages the giver's trust even when nothing has gone wrong.

That is the practical hinge. A church that can say plainly what came in, what it was spent on, and what happened to the money it asked for last time has already removed most of the pressure it would otherwise have to apply. Trust does the work urgency would otherwise be recruited to do.

Underneath all of it is a view of what generosity is for. Writing about those with means, Paul does not tell Timothy to work on their giving levels. He tells him to charge them "not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy," and then, "They are to do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share" (1 Timothy 6:17–18, ESV). The concern is where their hope is resting. Generosity is the evidence, not the objective. Teaching aimed at the budget produces gifts; teaching aimed at where a person's security sits produces givers, and the second outlasts the first — for much the same reason that [work built around one person eventually runs out](/blog/work-that-outlives-you).

So the test is not whether the appeal worked. It is whether the person who gave is freer than they were before. Say honestly what is needed and why. Account for the last gift without being asked. Make it easy to give a little without feeling small, and easy to give nothing this month without feeling watched. Then leave the decision where Paul left it, which is not in the room at all.
