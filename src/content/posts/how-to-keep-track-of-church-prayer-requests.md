It is Tuesday, and the church office is trying to reconstruct what was prayed for on Sunday.

The requests came in through five doors. One on a card dropped in the offering basket. One as a text to the pastor's personal number at eleven at night. One as a comment under the announcement post, three replies deep. Two names in the margin of a service sheet now in a bag in the back of a car. And one said out loud in the meeting, remembered afterwards by four people in four different versions.

None of that is neglect; it is what happens when a church collects something important without deciding where it goes. And prayer is the easiest thing in the world to collect carelessly, because asking for it is an act of trust and nobody wants to answer trust with a form.

## What actually breaks

**The list becomes a graveyard.** A prayer list that is never cleared is read once and then skimmed forever. By month three it carries a surgery that went fine in March, a job search that ended in April, and a marriage nobody is sure is still in difficulty. The stale entries are not clutter; they teach the reader that none of it is current.

**The person who asked never hears anything.** They put something frightening into the church's hands, and then silence. They do not need a report; they need to know it did not vanish.

**And the request is sitting wherever it happened to land.** A basket, a phone, a thread — not one of them chosen as the place to keep something sensitive. Nobody decided who could see it, whether it could be asked without a name on it, or when it would be cleared.

## The part that is not a software problem

A prayer request is usually two sensitive things at once. Under EU data protection law, processing personal data revealing "religious or philosophical beliefs" is prohibited, and so is processing "data concerning health" — both sit in the same list in Article 9(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679. A request about a diagnosis is both at once.

Article 9(2) sets out conditions that lift the prohibition, and two describe exactly what a church is doing. Point (a) is the data subject's "explicit consent to the processing of those personal data for one or more specified purposes." Point (d) covers a not-for-profit body "with a political, philosophical, religious or trade union aim," processing "in the course of its legitimate activities with appropriate safeguards," about its own members, former members, or people in regular contact with it — on the condition that the data are not disclosed outside that body without consent.

That last condition is the one a public post fails, and it fails it instantly.

The same regulation names a second principle worth borrowing anywhere. Article 5(1)(e), "storage limitation," requires personal data to be "kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed."

A church outside the EU and UK is not bound by either article, but together they are the clearest short statement of careful handling: ask before you collect, keep it inside the body, do not keep it longer than needed. (Both read 22 October 2026 — [Article 9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/9/adopted) and [Article 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/5/adopted/data.html).)

## A checklist for your church, whatever you run it on

1. **Decide who can see a request before you collect one.** The answer can be "the whole congregation" — it just has to be a decision somebody made and can say out loud.
2. **Give people a way to ask without their name on it.** Some of the most urgent requests in any church will never be made otherwise.
3. **Set a retention period, and say it before they post.** Thirty days is a reasonable default. Telling people in advance makes clearing the list an act of care rather than a deletion.
4. **Close the loop.** Some signal back to the person who asked that others actually prayed. A number is enough; silence is the one answer that does damage.
5. **Keep the detail out of anything public.** "Pray for the Mensah family this week" carries the request. The diagnosis does not need to travel with it.
6. **One place, not five.** The doors in the opening paragraph are the problem; any single place beats four good ones.

None of that requires software, and a church that does only it is already ahead of most.
