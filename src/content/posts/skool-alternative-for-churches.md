No church runs on one tool.

It runs on a member database, a giving page, kids' check-in, a community space, courses, a blog and tax receipts in January. That is the real list, and it is why most churches searching for a Skool alternative are not unhappy with Skool. They are unhappy that Skool covers two rows of that list beautifully and leaves the rest to be bought, wired together and paid for separately. Harvest is built the other way round: one subscription that carries the whole list, with one member record underneath it.

So this is not a feature-by-feature scorecard. It is one platform against an assembled stack, and what each of them costs a church every month. Competitor prices were read from each vendor's own pricing page on the date given.

## What Skool covers

Skool is a community platform, and a good one. Read from [skool.com/pricing](https://www.skool.com/pricing) on September 15, 2026: Hobby is **$9/month** and Pro is **$99/month**. Both list unlimited members, unlimited courses, unlimited videos, unlimited live calls and a custom URL. The difference between them is the money: Hobby carries a **10% transaction fee** on what you collect through Skool, Pro carries **2.9%**.

For a group that meets online, posts a weekly thread and runs a course, that is a strong product — and it is also the whole product. Skool's pricing page sells community, courses, video and calls. A member database built for a congregation, Sunday check-in for children, a giving page, year-end giving statements — none of those appear on it, because they are not what Skool is. A church that needs them buys a church management system as well, and that is where the bill starts moving.

## So here is what a church actually pays

Say a church is already running its people on Skool Pro and now wants the rest of the list: somewhere to hold member records, check children in on a Sunday, take gifts and issue statements in January. The usual answer is a ChMS. [Breeze ChMS](https://www.breezechms.com/pricing) is **$72/month** flat, read September 15, 2026 — one rate, no tiers by contact count, with giving, check-in and name tags, event management and a mobile app included.

Add it up. [Skool](https://www.skool.com/pricing) Pro at $99 plus [Breeze](https://www.breezechms.com/pricing) at $72 — both read September 15, 2026 — is **$171 a month, across two vendors, on two separate monthly bills.**

Harvest's Ministry plan is **$60/mo** on the monthly term — a third of that $171, compared like for like — or **$47/mo, billed as $564 every 12 months** on a yearly commitment. Skool discounts a year too, advertising two months free on its own yearly toggle, so monthly against monthly is the fair comparison. Either way it is one plan and one bill, carrying the community feed, 15 courses, the CRM to 2,000 contacts, QR check-in, the giving page, event registration, giving statements, the accounting ledger, the blog and custom branding.

A church under a hundred people usually does not need Ministry. Small Team is **$40/mo** monthly, or **$31.67/mo, billed as $380 every 12 months**, and it carries the community feed, the CRM to 500 contacts, five courses, QR check-in, livestream, the church map, the blog and the mobile app. Set $40 against $171 on the same monthly terms and it is under a quarter of the price — and it already does the Sunday morning job Skool was never built for.

## The fee line, stated plainly

Harvest's platform fee is **0% on every paid plan**. Not a promotional rate and not a tier benefit — it is zero on Individual, Small Team and Ministry alike.

The reason it can be zero is worth understanding. Harvest does not sit between a church and a gift at all: the church publishes its own PayPal, Cash App, Venmo, Zelle, Revolut or Wise links on one giving page in the app, and the money moves from the giver to the account the church already owns. There is nothing for Harvest to route, hold or take a percentage of. Whatever the church's own payment provider charges to move that money is between the church and that provider — a real cost, and not ours to quote.

Skool's fee works the other way. On Hobby, 10% of everything collected through the platform stays with the platform: a church running a $2,000 course cohort there hands over $200, on top of whatever the card costs.

Where Breeze genuinely does something Harvest does not: Breeze's own pricing page lists online and text giving, so a gift is processed inside the same system that records it, and Harvest has no card rail of its own. That is one row of the table — and the community space, the courses, the blog and the January statements still have to come from somewhere, at $99 a month and rising.

## The cost that never appears on an invoice

Two subscriptions is two bills. The more expensive problem is that it is also two lists of people.

A family joins the church. Someone adds them to Breeze. Do they appear in Skool? Only if a volunteer remembers. Six months in, the member records live in one system and the course progress in another, and nobody can answer "who has been in our community all year and has never been asked to give?" without exporting two CSVs and matching them by hand on a Tuesday night.

Then there are the parts nobody budgets: the volunteer hours spent reconciling, the integration that breaks because a vendor shipped on a Thursday, the second login every leader has to be issued and eventually revoked, and the January scramble to build [giving statements](/blog/year-end-giving-statements-what-to-include) out of records that were never in one place.

One platform means one member record. Giving, check-in history, group, course progress and prayer requests are attributes of the same person, because there is only one person for them to be attributes of.

## What we would actually say

Harvest's argument has never been that it out-features anybody. It is that a church's software bill and a church's member list should each be one thing rather than several, and that nothing on the list above is worth a second vendor.

Skool builds a good community and a good course library, and nothing here has argued otherwise. What this post has argued is that almost no church stops there. The children's ministry needs a check-in. The treasurer needs statements. The events come, then the member database, then the blog — and each one arrives as a separate decision, a separate invoice and a separate list of names. Harvest is the bet that a church would rather make that decision once, and keep every one of those people in a single record.
