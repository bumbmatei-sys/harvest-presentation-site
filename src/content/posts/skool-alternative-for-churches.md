No church runs on one tool.

It runs on a member database, a giving page, kids' check-in, a community space, courses, a blog and tax receipts in January. That is the real list, and it is why most churches searching for a Skool alternative are not unhappy with Skool. They are unhappy that Skool covers two rows of it and leaves the rest to be bought, wired together and paid for separately. Harvest is built the other way round: one subscription carrying the whole list, with one member record underneath it.

So this is not a feature-by-feature scorecard. It is one platform against an assembled stack, and what each costs a month. Competitor prices come from each vendor's own pricing page, on the date given.

## What Skool covers

Skool is a community platform. Read from [skool.com/pricing](https://www.skool.com/pricing) on September 15, 2026: Hobby is **$9/month** and Pro is **$99/month**. Both list unlimited members, unlimited courses, unlimited videos, unlimited live calls and a custom URL. The difference between them is the money: Hobby carries a **10% transaction fee** on what you collect through Skool, Pro carries **2.9%**.

That is the product, and it is also all of it. Skool's pricing page sells community, courses, video and calls. A member database built for a congregation, Sunday check-in, a giving page, year-end statements — none of those appear on it, because they are not what Skool is. A church that needs them buys a church management system as well, and that is where the bill starts moving.

## So here is what a church actually pays

Say a church on Skool Pro now wants the rest of the list: member records, Sunday check-in, gifts, statements in January. The usual answer is a church management system alongside it, and Planning Center is the generous end of that market. Read September 15, 2026: [planningcenter.com/pricing](https://www.planningcenter.com/pricing) gives People, the member database, free and unlimited, with a free tier on each other product. [Planning Center Check-Ins](https://www.planningcenter.com/check-ins) is free to 10 daily check-ins, then **$15/month** at 30. [Planning Center Giving](https://www.planningcenter.com/giving) is free to 10 donations a month, then **$15/month** at 75, and processes US cards at 2.15% + $0.30.

Add it up. [Skool](https://www.skool.com/pricing) Pro at $99, plus [Planning Center Giving](https://www.planningcenter.com/giving) at $15 and [Planning Center Check-Ins](https://www.planningcenter.com/check-ins) at $15 — all read September 15, 2026 — is **$129 a month, across two vendors, on two separate bills.** That figure is deliberately conservative: it does not price the blog or website tool the table's last row still calls for.

Harvest's Ministry plan is **$80/mo** on the monthly term — $49 a month under that $129, compared like for like — or **$62.67/mo, billed as $752 every 12 months** on a yearly commitment. Skool discounts a year too, advertising two months free on its own yearly toggle, so monthly against monthly is the fair comparison. Either way it is one plan and one bill, carrying the community feed, 15 courses, the CRM to 2,000 contacts, QR check-in, the giving page, event registration, giving statements, the blog and custom branding.

A church under a hundred people usually does not need Ministry. Small Team is **$40/mo** monthly, or **$31.67/mo, billed as $380 every 12 months**, carrying the community feed, the CRM to 500 contacts, five courses, QR check-in, livestream, the church map, the blog and the mobile app. Match that scope on the other side — Skool Pro at $99, Planning Center Check-Ins at $15 — and it is $114 against $40, on the same monthly terms.

## The fee line, stated plainly

Harvest's platform fee is **0% on every paid plan** — zero on Individual, Small Team and Ministry alike, not a promotional rate and not a tier benefit.

It can be zero because Harvest does not sit between a church and a gift at all: the church publishes its own PayPal, Cash App, Venmo, Zelle, Revolut or Wise links on one giving page in the app, and the money moves from the giver to the account the church already owns. There is nothing for Harvest to route, hold or take a share of. What the church's own payment provider charges is a real cost, and not ours to quote.

Skool's fee works the other way. On Hobby, 10% of everything collected through the platform stays there: a $2,000 course cohort hands over $200, on top of the card cost.

Planning Center Giving does one thing here Harvest does not: it processes the card itself, at 2.15% + $0.30 in the US, so the gift and its receipt are one movement in one system. That is one row of the table, and the community space, the courses and the blog still have to come from somewhere at $99 a month.

## The cost that never appears on an invoice

Two subscriptions is two bills. The more expensive problem is that it is two lists of people.

A family joins the church. Someone adds them to Planning Center People. Do they appear in Skool? Only if a volunteer remembers. Six months in, the member records live in one system and the course progress in another, and nobody can answer "who has been in our community all year and has never been asked to give?" without exporting two CSVs and matching them by hand.

Then there are the parts nobody budgets: the hours spent reconciling, the integration that breaks because a vendor shipped on a Thursday, the second login every leader is issued and revoked, the January scramble to build [giving statements](/blog/year-end-giving-statements-what-to-include) out of records that were never in one place.

One platform means one member record. Giving, check-in history, group, course progress and prayer requests are attributes of the same person, because there is only one person for them to be attributes of.

## What we would actually say

Harvest's argument has never been that it out-features anybody. It is that a church's software bill and a church's member list should each be one thing rather than several.

Skool builds a good community and a good course library, and nothing here has argued otherwise. What this post has argued is that almost no church stops there. The children's ministry needs a check-in. The treasurer needs statements. The events come, then the member database, then the blog — each one a separate decision, a separate invoice and a separate list of names. Harvest is the bet that a church would rather make that decision once, and keep every one of those people in a single record.

The same arithmetic, run against a church management system instead, is in our [earlier comparison](/blog/planning-center-alternative-small-churches); what sits in each Harvest plan is on the [pricing page](/#pricing).

One subscription. One member record. One login. That is the whole of Harvest's argument, and it is the whole of this post.
